## SLIDE 2: Introduction [1 minute]

"So first, some context. A Medical Representative, or MR, is someone
who works for a pharma company and visits doctors every day to promote
medicines. India alone has over 5 lakh MRs.

A typical MR visits 8 to 12 doctors in a single day. Each doctor has
a different location, different availability window, and different
importance level.

On the left side you can see what a typical MR day looks like.
On the right side, you can see why this is harder than it sounds.

Every doctor has a different schedule. Some are high-value, some are
not. Travel between locations eats into productive time. And right now,
most MRs just plan by habit, not by data.

Our project tackles exactly this: which doctors to visit, when, and
in what order."


## SLIDE 3: Problem Statement [1 minute]

"Currently, MRs plan using spreadsheets, memory, or routes they have
always followed. This ignores availability, priority, and distance.

The specific problems this causes are listed here:
- No scoring mechanism, so high and low priority doctors get equal time
- Routes are habitual, not optimized
- Availability is tracked informally, so MRs show up when the doctor
  is not available
- Managers have no real visibility into what is happening in the field

Our problem statement is: How can we use AI-based priority prediction
and constraint-based route optimization to automate daily visit
planning for Medical Representatives?"


## SLIDE 4: System Overview [1.5 minutes]

"Here is the complete pipeline. I will walk through it left to right.

We start with historical data: doctor profiles, past visit records,
and appointment information. This goes through preprocessing and
feature engineering.

Then the Random Forest model predicts a priority score between 0 and 1
for each doctor. A score close to 1 means high priority.

Those scores, along with real-world constraints like doctor availability
windows and MR working hours, go into Google OR-Tools.

OR-Tools solves what is called a Vehicle Routing Problem with Time
Windows, or VRPTW. It finds the best sequence of visits that respects
all the constraints while minimizing travel.

The table below shows the three layers. Prediction layer scores the
visits. Constraint layer applies the rules. Optimization layer
sequences the route. Each layer is independent and testable on its own."

[If asked: "What is VRPTW?" say: "It is a standard optimization
problem. Given a set of locations with time windows and travel costs,
find the minimum-cost route that visits all of them within their
windows. It is NP-hard, so OR-Tools uses a heuristic called guided
local search to find good solutions quickly."]

## SLIDE 7: Architecture [1 minute]

"The architecture is layered.

At the top, three user types: MR, Doctor, and Manager. They interact
through a React.js frontend with role-based views.

The frontend talks to a FastAPI backend through REST APIs. The backend
handles authentication, business logic, and orchestrates the ML model
and the optimizer.

At the bottom, three services: MySQL for data storage, Random Forest
for priority prediction, and Google OR-Tools for route optimization.

The key architectural decision is that the ML model and the optimizer
are separate modules. The model scores visits, the optimizer sequences
them. We can test, tune, or replace either one independently."

[If asked "Why FastAPI?": "Async support, so the API does not block
while the optimizer runs. Auto-generated documentation. Built-in
type validation with Pydantic."]

## SLIDE 9: Design / Algorithms / Techniques [1.5 minutes]

"Two core algorithms.

First, Random Forest. It is an ensemble of decision trees, typically
100 to 500 trees. Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample of the
data. The final prediction is by majority vote. The reason we chose
Random Forest is that it gives us feature importance rankings. When
someone asks 'why is Doctor A ranked higher?', we can point to
specific features like days since last visit or prescription volume.

Second, VRPTW solved by Google OR-Tools. Given locations, travel
times, service durations, and time windows, OR-Tools finds the
minimum-cost route. It uses a heuristic called guided local search.

The table at the bottom shows our feature engineering pipeline. Six
features: days since last visit, visit frequency, doctor specialty,
prescription volume, geographic distance, and availability window."

[If asked "Why not neural networks?": "Random Forest works well on
tabular data of this size and gives interpretable results. Neural
networks lose interpretability and are overkill for our dataset scale."]

[If asked "Why not XGBoost?": "We plan to benchmark against XGBoost.
Random Forest is our primary model because of interpretability."]

## SLIDE 11: Proposed System [1 minute]

"This is the step-by-step workflow from the MR's perspective.

Step 1: MR logs in. System pulls territory data.
Step 2 and 3: Data gets cleaned and features are extracted.
Step 4: Random Forest scores each doctor with a priority.
Step 5: Constraints are applied: this doctor only available 10 to 12,
that visit needs 30 minutes, MR works 9 to 6.
Step 6: OR-Tools produces the optimized sequence.
Step 7: MR sees the plan on the dashboard with visit order and
estimated arrival times.

The four differentiators at the bottom are important: data-driven
priority instead of gut feel, time-window aware routing, integrated
pipeline, and manager visibility."

# QUICK REFERENCE: COMMON EXAMINER QUESTIONS

Q: "What is the difference between your system and Google Maps?"
A: "Google Maps finds a route between two points. It cannot decide
which doctors to visit first, it cannot factor in priority scores,
and it cannot handle time-window constraints. Our system handles
selection and sequencing together."

Q: "Is VRPTW NP-hard?"
A: "Yes. That is why we use OR-Tools, which uses guided local search,
a metaheuristic that finds good solutions in reasonable time. For
10-15 doctors per day, it solves in seconds."

Q: "What if the ML model predicts wrong?"
A: "The priority score is a weight, not a hard constraint. Even if
it is slightly off, the optimizer still produces a valid route. Worst
case is a suboptimal order, not a system failure."

Q: "What dataset are you using?"
A: "We plan to use a combination of publicly available pharmaceutical
sales data and simulated visit records. For the ML model, we need
historical visit data with at least 6 months of records. If public
data is insufficient, we will generate synthetic data that matches
real-world distributions."

Q: "What accuracy do you expect?"
A: "Based on similar classification tasks, we target 80-90% accuracy.
But accuracy alone is not enough for imbalanced data. We will track
precision and recall for the high-priority class specifically."

Q: "Why not use a genetic algorithm instead of OR-Tools?"
A: "OR-Tools has built-in VRPTW support with constraint handling.
Implementing our own GA would add complexity without clear benefit
at our scale."

Q: "What happens if a doctor cancels mid-day?"
A: "In current scope, re-optimization is triggered manually. The
system re-runs OR-Tools with updated constraints. Real-time
automatic re-routing is future scope."

Q: "How are you splitting work among 3 people?"
A: "One handles ML model and preprocessing. One handles constraint
engine and route optimization. One handles frontend and authentication.
Database design and integration testing are shared."
